Why this matters

Reliable delivery underpins trade competitiveness, public procurement and the supply of essential goods. Late shipments create contractual penalties, raise cost and reduce confidence in logistics corridors. This study measures how reliably one large global operator meets its delivery commitments and identifies where intervention would have the greatest effect.

Key findings

  1. The main cause of delay is unrealistic service promises, not geography. Shipping mode explains most of the variation in lateness. Market, customer type, product and payment method have no meaningful effect.
  2. Premium services fail most often. First Class is late on 100% of orders: it is consistently delivered in 2 days against a 1-day promise. Second Class is late on 79.8% of orders because its real transit time (2–6 days) is the same as Standard Class.
  3. Standard Class performs best (60.2% within schedule), and its late orders are limited to shipments taking 5–6 days against a 4-day promise.
  4. Regional differences are real but modest. Late rates range from 51.6% (Canada) to 60.1% (Central Africa). Western Europe, and Germany in particular, combines above-average delay with very high volume.
  5. Business and individual customers are affected equally (57.1–57.6% late), so enterprise contracts carry the same breach rate as consumer orders.

Bottom line: Aligning delivery promises with real network capability would raise on-time performance from 43% to about 60–68% almost immediately. Building genuinely faster premium lanes could take it to about 74%.

Recommended actions

  • Immediate (0–3 months): Re-quote First Class at 2 days, or launch a true next-day lane. Stop selling Second Class as a 2-day service until it is operationally separated from Standard Class.
  • Short term (3–6 months): Introduce day-3 exception alerts for Standard Class. Give proactive status updates to corporate and public-sector contract shipments.
  • Medium term (6–12 months): Carry out lane reviews in Central Africa, Western Europe/Germany, South Asia and watch-list countries (Malaysia, Chile, DR Congo, South Korea, Peru, Austria), covering carrier and customs dwell time.
  • Data and governance: Publish the five delivery KPIs on the live dashboard each week. Add shipment dates, carrier and warehouse identifiers to the data feed so that predictive early-warning models can be built.

Policy relevance

For public bodies that procure logistics services or oversee trade corridors, the findings support three practices: (1) require contracted SLAs to be backed by evidence of achieved transit times; (2) ask for regular reporting of on-time rate and average delay by service level and region; and (3) prioritise corridor-improvement investment where region-level delay indices are consistently above average.

Method: 180,519 order records; 7,754 cancelled shipments excluded. Delay Gap = actual − scheduled shipping days. Statistical significance assessed with chi-square tests. The full research paper, code and the interactive dashboard are available in the project repository.
